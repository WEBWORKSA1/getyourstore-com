A new store has no reviews, no search rankings and no audience. Paid ads can feel like the obvious fix, but with no proof the offer converts, they're an expensive way to learn. The first 100 customers usually come from doing things that don't scale. Here's the order that works.

## 1. Your warm network (customers 1–10)

Before launch, write a personal message to people who know you: friends, family, colleagues and past clients. Don't spam; explain what you've built and why, and ask for two things: an honest opinion and, if it's right for them, a purchase. Ask your first buyers for reviews and photos. Those are worth more than the revenue.

## 2. Pre-launch waitlist (customers 10–30)

Put up a "coming soon" page with an email signup weeks before launch, ideally with an incentive such as early access or a launch discount. Share it everywhere you can. On launch day, email the list first. A warm list converts far better than cold traffic.

## 3. Communities where your customers already gather (customers 20–50)

Find the forums, groups, subreddits and local communities where your ideal customers spend time. Then:

- **Contribute first.** Answer questions and share expertise for weeks before mentioning your store.
- **Follow each community's rules** on self-promotion.
- **Share stories, not ads**: why you made the product, what you learned, behind-the-scenes photos.

## 4. Content that helps (customers 30–70)

Create short, useful content around your product's problem: how-tos, comparisons, behind-the-scenes, before-and-afters. Short-form video is particularly effective for discovery. Every piece should point to a relevant product or your email signup.

## 5. Collaborations and creators

Partner with micro-creators (small, engaged audiences in your niche). Offer free product, a commission, or both. Complementary small brands are great partners too: a candle maker and a ceramicist can run a joint giveaway or bundle. Always make sure partnerships are disclosed according to advertising rules.

## 6. Search: start early, compound later

SEO is slow but compounding. From day one, write product titles and descriptions for search, add a few helpful guides on your blog and set up Google Search Console. Register for free product listings where available, such as Google Merchant Center. See [ecommerce SEO basics](/guides/ecommerce-seo-basics.html).

## 7. Email: your highest-return channel

Every visitor who doesn't buy today might buy later, if you capture their email. Set up:

- a signup form with a clear incentive
- a welcome series (story, best sellers, social proof, offer)
- abandoned-cart emails
- a post-purchase series asking for reviews and suggesting related products

## 8. Local and offline

Pop-up markets, local shops (consignment or wholesale), events and local press can drive early sales and real conversations with customers. If you sell in person, use a platform with point-of-sale integration so inventory stays in sync.

## 9. Paid ads, once the offer is proven

Once you have organic sales, reviews and a product page that converts, small paid tests make sense:

1. Calculate your **break-even ROAS** and **maximum cost per acquisition** with the [break-even calculator](/tools/break-even-calculator.html).
2. Start with a small daily budget on one channel.
3. Test one variable at a time (audience, creative, offer).
4. Kill what loses money quickly; scale what beats break-even.

## Track the right numbers

Weekly, track: visitors, conversion rate, average order value, revenue per visitor, email subscribers and repeat purchase rate. Revenue per visitor is the single best summary of how your store is performing.

## The real secret

Talk to your customers. Email every early buyer personally, ask why they bought and what nearly stopped them, then fix it. The insights from your first 100 customers will shape your next 10,000.
