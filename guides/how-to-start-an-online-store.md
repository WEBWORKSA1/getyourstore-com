Starting an online store is mostly a sequence of decisions made in the right order. Most new founders get stuck because they start in the middle: they pick a theme before they know what they sell, or buy inventory before they know anyone wants it. This guide walks through the sequence that works, with the reasoning behind each step.

## 1. Start with a problem, not a product

The strongest stores solve a specific problem for a specific group of people: "comfortable work shoes for nurses on 12-hour shifts" beats "shoes". Write a one-sentence description of who you serve and what you help them do. If you can't, you aren't ready to build yet.

Then look for evidence of demand:

- **Search interest.** Use Google Trends and the "People also ask" box to see whether people are actively looking.
- **Competitors.** Competition is a good sign: it proves demand. Find five competitors and read their one- to three-star reviews. Complaints are your product roadmap.
- **Direct signals.** Collect emails with a simple "notify me" page, pre-sell a small batch, or ask in communities where your customers already gather.

Twenty strangers who give you their email address is a stronger signal than two hundred friends who say "great idea".

## 2. Choose a business model

How you source and deliver products shapes your margins, cash needs and workload:

| Model | Upfront cash | Margin | Control | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hold inventory | Higher | Highest | Full | Brands with a proven product |
| Dropshipping | Low | Thin | Low | Testing demand quickly |
| Print-on-demand | Very low | Moderate | Medium | Design-led merch |
| Digital products | Very low | Very high | Full | Knowledge, templates, media |

We compare these in depth in [dropshipping vs inventory vs print-on-demand](/guides/dropshipping-vs-inventory-vs-print-on-demand.html).

## 3. Run the numbers before you build

Work out your all-in cost per order: product cost, packaging, shipping, payment fees, platform fees and the ad spend needed to win the sale. Then decide your price. If the margin after all of that is thin, the business will struggle however good the website is. The [profit margin calculator](/tools/profit-margin-calculator.html) does this in a minute.

Next, estimate startup costs (platform, domain, design, photography, starting stock and marketing) and keep three months of running costs in reserve. Most stores take 60 to 90 days to find a marketing channel that works reliably.

## 4. Pick a platform that fits

There are four broad options:

- **Hosted platforms** (Shopify, BigCommerce, Wix, Squarespace, Square Online): easiest to run, with security and uptime handled for you, for a monthly fee.
- **Open-source** (WooCommerce on WordPress): maximum control and ownership, but you look after hosting and updates.
- **Marketplaces** (Etsy, Amazon): built-in buyers, but less brand control and you don't own the customer relationship.
- **Creator platforms** (Payhip, Sellfy, Gumroad): fastest for digital products.

Choose based on what you sell and how much maintenance you want, not on hype. Our [platform guide](/guides/choose-ecommerce-platform.html) and [60-second quiz](/tools/platform-quiz.html) make it quick.

## 5. Name your store and secure the domain

Pick something short, easy to say and broad enough to grow into. Check the matching .com, social handles and your country's trademark database before you commit. See [how to name your store](/guides/store-name-and-domain.html).

## 6. Build the storefront

Keep the first version simple:

- A fast, mobile-first theme; most visitors will be on a phone.
- A homepage with one clear promise, one strong image and one button.
- Navigation with no more than five or six top-level items.
- An About page with a real person and a real story.

Resist the urge to install a dozen apps. Each one adds cost and can slow your pages down.

## 7. Create product pages that sell

Your product pages do most of the selling. Each one needs a searchable title, benefit-led copy, at least three consistent photos, clear pricing and variants, shipping and returns information, and answers to common questions. Our guide to [product pages that convert](/guides/product-pages-that-convert.html) covers each element.

## 8. Set up payments, shipping and tax

Connect a payment processor and offer popular wallets where available. Set shipping zones and rates, decide whether to offer free shipping above a threshold, and configure sales tax, VAT or GST for the places you sell. Then **place a real test order on your phone**. You'll catch problems no checklist can. Details in [payments, shipping and tax basics](/guides/payments-shipping-taxes-basics.html).

## 9. Publish your policies

At minimum, publish a privacy policy, terms of service, a refund and returns policy, a shipping policy and a contact page. These protect you, are required by many payment processors and ad platforms, and reassure buyers. See [legal pages for online stores](/guides/legal-pages-for-online-stores.html).

## 10. Launch, then market relentlessly

Launch to the people who already know you first, then work outward: communities, content, search, email, collaborations and only then paid ads. The [first 100 customers playbook](/guides/first-100-customers.html) lays out the order.

> The best time to launch is about 4–6 weeks before a peak sales date. That gives you time to gather reviews, fix issues and test ads before demand spikes. Our homepage shows a live countdown to the next one.

## A realistic timeline

With products ready, a focused founder can go from idea to a live store in two to four weeks: one week to validate and plan, one week to build and write product pages, and a few days to test and launch. The marketing work starts on launch day and never really stops.
