How you get products to customers is one of the biggest decisions you'll make. It determines how much cash you need, what margins are possible, how much control you have over quality and how much work lands on your desk each day. Here's how the three main models compare.

## Holding your own inventory

You buy stock up front (from a manufacturer, wholesaler or your own workshop), store it and ship orders yourself or through a fulfilment partner (a 3PL).

**Pros**

- The best margins, because you buy in bulk
- Full control over quality, packaging and delivery speed
- A real, defensible brand, especially with your own or exclusive products

**Cons**

- Cash is tied up in stock before you've made a sale
- Risk of unsold inventory
- Storage, packing and shipping take time or cost money

**Best for:** products you've already validated, private-label brands, handmade goods, and anyone who wants to build long-term brand value.

## Dropshipping

You list products from a supplier, and when a customer orders, the supplier ships directly to them. You never touch the stock.

**Pros**

- Very low upfront cost
- Huge product range and easy testing
- Location-independent

**Cons**

- Thin margins after product cost, ads and fees
- Little control over quality, packaging and shipping times
- Fierce competition, because other stores often sell the identical product
- Customer service problems are yours even when the supplier causes them

**Best for:** testing demand cheaply before investing in stock, and adding complementary products to an existing brand. If you dropship, choose suppliers with reliable shipping times, order samples first and be transparent about delivery estimates.

## Print-on-demand (POD)

Your designs are printed on products (t-shirts, mugs, posters, phone cases) only when someone orders, then shipped by the POD provider.

**Pros**

- No inventory and very low risk
- Unique products through your own designs
- Easy to launch many variations

**Cons**

- Moderate margins, since per-unit costs are higher than bulk
- Limited control over production and delivery times
- The design and the audience are everything; the blanks are commodities

**Best for:** artists, creators and communities with a following, niche humour and interest-based brands, and merch for an existing audience.

## Side-by-side

| | Inventory | Dropshipping | Print-on-demand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upfront cash | High | Low | Very low |
| Typical margin | Highest | Lowest | Middle |
| Quality control | Full | Low | Medium |
| Shipping speed control | Full | Low | Medium |
| Brand defensibility | Strong | Weak | Medium (via designs) |
| Daily workload | Higher | Lower | Lower |

## Don't forget digital products

If you can package knowledge or creativity (templates, presets, courses, patterns, printables), digital products have near-zero delivery cost and very high margins. See [selling digital products](/guides/selling-digital-products.html).

## How to choose

1. **Check the maths.** Put realistic numbers into the [profit margin calculator](/tools/profit-margin-calculator.html), including ad cost per order. If the margin is under about 15–20% after everything, the model probably won't sustain paid marketing.
2. **Match it to your cash.** Little capital? Start with POD, dropshipping or pre-orders. Validated demand and capital? Inventory.
3. **Match it to your strength.** A great designer or creator? POD. A product sourcing expert? Inventory. Strong at marketing and testing? Dropshipping can validate ideas fast.

## The hybrid path many brands follow

Many successful stores **start lean and graduate**: they test with dropshipping, POD or pre-orders, then move winning products to bulk inventory or private label once demand is proven. This keeps early risk low while still building towards better margins and a real brand.
